<section id="bwb" class="case-study">
  <header class="case-study-header">
    <div>
      <p class="eyebrow dark">FLAGSHIP AIRCRAFT PROJECT · JAN–APR 2026</p>
      <h2>Blended-Wing-Body Aerodynamics</h2>
      <p class="case-study-subtitle">Geometry design · CFD solution verification · FDM fabrication · force-balance test · tuft flow visualization</p>
    </div>
    <a class="text-link" href="assets/BWB_Project_Showcase_Tufts%20(4).html">Full technical showcase ↗</a>
  </header>

  <div class="case-hero">
    <img src="assets/IMG_3685 (1).jpeg" alt="3D-printed BWB model mounted to a three-component wind tunnel balance">
    <div class="case-hero-caption">
      <span>EWT 18 × 18 in test section</span>
      <span>5.0% blockage</span>
      <span>V∞ ≈ 40 mph</span>
      <span>Re ≈ 5.6 × 10⁴</span>
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="metric-grid four case-metrics">
    <div class="metric"><strong>7.31</strong><span>peak CFD L/D at 6°</span></div>
    <div class="metric"><strong>14–16°</strong><span>stall onset in tunnel data</span></div>
    <div class="metric"><strong>3.36 N</strong><span>peak normal force at 18°</span></div>
    <div class="metric"><strong>&lt;0.5%</strong><span>domain sensitivity in CL/CD</span></div>
  </div>

  <div class="case-intro">
    <p>
      I built this project as a complete design-to-test loop rather than a standalone CFD exercise.
      I defined a custom blended-wing-body outer mold line in SOLIDWORKS, established a repeatable pre-stall CFD workflow,
      fabricated a 1:1 FDM model, and extended the test campaign through 20° angle of attack using a three-component balance and tuft visualization.
    </p>
    <p>
      The main engineering question was not simply “what is the lift coefficient?” but whether the analysis and test workflow could reveal
      how a blended planform builds load, loses efficiency, and separates as angle of attack increases.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="case-section">
    <div class="case-section-copy">
      <p class="section-number">01 / GEOMETRY</p>
      <h3>Designed the outer mold line from cross-sections to test article</h3>
      <p>
        The planform spans approximately 8 in (0.203 m), is 9 in (0.229 m) long, and reaches roughly 2 in (0.051 m) peak thickness.
        A multi-section loft with four guide curves blends the centerbody into the outer panels. I defined a custom centerbody section to provide
        the volume and smooth spanwise blending needed by the configuration while maintaining a clean leading edge and tapered trailing edge.
      </p>
      <div class="spec-row">
        <span><strong>Sref</strong> 9.62 × 10⁻³ m²</span>
        <span><strong>c̄</strong> ≈ 0.047 m</span>
        <span><strong>q∞</strong> ≈ 192 Pa</span>
      </div>
    </div>
    <div class="media-grid two">
      <figure class="figure-card">
        <img src="assets/Cadbwbsketch.png" alt="BWB concept sketch and geometry development">
        <figcaption>Concept and early geometry definition.</figcaption>
      </figure>
      <figure class="figure-card">
        <img src="assets/BWB_Aero_CADd1.png" alt="SolidWorks loft used to construct the BWB outer mold line">
        <figcaption>SOLIDWORKS multi-section loft and guide-curve definition.</figcaption>
      </figure>
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="case-section">
    <div class="case-section-copy">
      <p class="section-number">02 / CFD SOLUTION VERIFICATION</p>
      <h3>Built a repeatable low-Reynolds-number CFD workflow before using the sweep for design conclusions</h3>
      <p>
        Steady external-flow cases used a fixed geometry with the inlet velocity vector rotated to impose angle of attack.
        The sweep covered −2° to +8° at 40 mph with turbulence enabled (1% intensity, 0.01 m length scale).
        Each case continued for 100 additional iterations after the goal criteria were marked achieved, and reported coefficients came from the stabilized averaging window.
      </p>
      <p>
        A domain study at 4° changed CL by about 0.17% and CD by about 0.43%. Medium-to-fine mesh refinement changed CL by about 1.25% and CD by about 0.37%,
        supporting the medium mesh for the sweep with a fine-mesh verification point.
      </p>
      <div class="callout">
        <strong>Terminology matters:</strong>
        these grid/domain studies are numerical <em>verification</em>. Experimental <em>validation</em> requires a controlled comparison to physical measurements,
        so the site keeps those claims separate.
      </div>
    </div>

  <div class="case-section">
    <div class="case-section-copy">
      <p class="section-number">03 / PRE-STALL PERFORMANCE + STALL CAMPAIGN</p>
      <h3>Used CFD for the pre-stall regime, then pushed the physical test 12° farther to capture the full stall sequence</h3>
      <p>
        The verified CFD sweep shows CL rising nearly linearly through 6°. L/D reaches 7.31 at 6° and declines by 8° as drag grows faster than lift.
        The wind-tunnel campaign then extends from −2° through 20° using the EWT three-component balance, wind-off tare, point-by-point dynamic-pressure correction,
        and 25 Hz acquisition.
      </p>
      <p>
        The tunnel data identifies a progressive stall onset around 14–16°: the normal-force increment collapses relative to the linear regime and pitching moment reverses near 16°.
        Normal force continues to rise to 3.36 N at 18° and falls only about 1% by 20°, indicating a soft, progressive loss of loading rather than an abrupt full-span break.
      </p>
    </div>

  <div class="case-section">
    <div class="case-section-copy">
      <p class="section-number">04 / FLOW VISUALIZATION</p>
      <h3>The force balance showed when stall began; tufts showed where it began</h3>
      <p>
        Approximately 7 mm yarn tufts at roughly 12% chord spacing were filmed from a fixed overhead view.
        The visual sequence shows the centerbody becoming disturbed before the outer panels, with clearly separated inboard flow at 14–16° while the tips remain partially attached.
        By 18–20°, the centerbody is broadly separated but the outer panels still retain some ordered flow.
      </p>
      <p>
        That spanwise progression explains the soft stall in the force data: load is shed progressively from the center outward instead of disappearing across the entire planform at once.
      </p>
    </div>

  <div class="finding-grid">
    <div class="finding">
      <span>01</span>
      <h4>Stall onset is progressive, not abrupt</h4>
      <p>Force and tuft evidence converge on a 14–16° onset window, followed by gradual spanwise separation.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="finding">
      <span>02</span>
      <h4>Best CFD efficiency occurs at 6°</h4>
      <p>Within the −2° to +8° simulated range, L/D peaks at 7.31 before drag growth overtakes lift growth.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="finding">
      <span>03</span>
      <h4>Pitching moment changes before peak normal force</h4>
      <p>The 16° pitching-moment reversal preceding the 18° force peak is consistent with a redistribution of aerodynamic loading near stall.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="finding">
      <span>04</span>
      <h4>The outer panels keep carrying load</h4>
      <p>Tufts remain partly ordered at the tips through 20°, explaining why the force curve rolls over instead of collapsing.</p>
    </div>
  </div>

  <header class="case-study-header">
    <div>
      <p class="eyebrow dark">RESEARCH · FLIGHT DYNAMICS</p>
      <h2>Learned Rotorcraft Dynamics for Controller-Facing Simulation</h2>
      <p class="case-study-subtitle">Physics-informed ML · rigid-body dynamics · rollout stability · closed-loop evaluation</p>
    </div>
    <span class="status-pill light">IEEE Aerospace 2027 · Accepted</span>
  </header>

  <div class="split-feature">
    <div>
      <p class="project-lede">
        I am developing learned rotorcraft dynamics models that predict forces and moments while preserving the rigid-body equations of motion.
        The research asks a stricter question than one-step prediction accuracy: does the learned model remain useful when it is rolled forward through a trajectory
        or inserted into a controller-facing simulation?
      </p>
      <ul class="clean-list">
        <li>Implemented the PyTorch flight-dynamics pipeline and force/moment integration.</li>
        <li>Compared feed-forward, temporal, recurrent, and training-objective variants under matched conditions.</li>
        <li>Built held-out trajectory rollout diagnostics for drift, local behavior, and control-relevant response.</li>
        <li>Extended evaluation toward stability and tracking rather than optimizing a single aggregate prediction metric.</li>
      </ul>
      <p class="paper-note">
        <strong>Accepted paper:</strong> “From Accurate Predictions to Controller-Facing Learned Rotorcraft Surrogates: Stability &amp; Tracking.”
      </p>
    </div>
